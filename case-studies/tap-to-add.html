The Build · The 3% Move

Same primitive. Different moment.

Stripe Terminal's Tap to Pay was built for merchants accepting payment. The hardware, the cryptogram, the card-present authentication path — all of it pointed at the moment of charging. We pointed it at the moment of saving. The infrastructure didn't change. What changed was what it was for.

Infrastructure can be repointed, not just built.

The Build · The Architecture

A SetupIntent with card_present. And everything that means.

The merchant calls a SetupIntent with the card_present payment method type. The Mobile Payment Element handles the buyer-facing flow. Under the hood, Stripe Terminal's SDK provides the cryptogram-backed NFC read, validates card-presence, and returns a PaymentMethod the merchant can use immediately or save for future charges.

Three things follow from the card_present classification: card-present interchange treatment, issuer-side liability for fraud, and CVC bypass eligibility on supported networks.

The Build · Tradeoffs Collapsed

Auth, fraud, CVC, effort. Normally a tradeoff. Not here.

Cutting CVC normally raises fraud. Raising auth normally requires more verification friction. Removing fields normally costs verification. Each pair is a known tradeoff in payments.

Tap to Add wins on all four simultaneously — and the only reason it can is the card-present primitive underneath. The economic asymmetry is the design.

The Build · Design Language

Two design languages. One buyer.

Stripe Terminal's tap screen was built for merchants. The Mobile Payment Element was built for buyers. The same physical gesture had to work in both worlds without feeling like two different products bolted together.

I led the cross-team alignment between OCS Mobile and Terminal — pushing for a centralized design language as the surfaces converged. We resolved the language for private preview and stood up a working group with Phil Machalski (Terminal Design) for the path to GA.

The Process · The Constraints

What private preview meant. And what it didn't.

Three known tradeoffs accepted at private preview, documented for GA.

01 · SDK Size

20–30MB at preview. Target: under 2MB at GA.

A lightweight NFC fallback is under exploration to scale Tap to Add to merchants who can't accept the full Terminal SDK binary.

02 · Bifurcated Tap Screen

"Enter card info manually" deferred to GA.

Limited time before Sessions meant the bifurcated tap screen wasn't feasible. Backup plan: Tap to Add is no longer the default when the buyer selects "Card," reducing surprise.

03 · Design Language

Working group standing up.

Terminal Design + OCS Mobile alignment was scoped, gaveled in part, and continues into GA. Started with Phil Machalski; broadens to dashboard team for the wider mobile ecosystem.

The Process · The Cross-Team Argument

Terminal's screen was for merchants. Ours wasn't.

Terminal's existing tap screen had been built and battle-tested for merchant-facing point-of-sale. Buyers in MPE land on a merchant screen if no design intervention happens. The "X" button takes them back to the card form (confusing). The animation isn't device-aware. The visual language belongs to a different product.

Three buyer-side recommendations went into the gaveled designs: a device-specific NFC animation, a clear "Enter card info manually" exit, and an "X" that returns to the payment methods screen. Two shipped at preview; one held for GA.

Sole designer doesn't mean solo work. Cross-team alignment was the highest-leverage thing I did this project.

The Process · The Private Preview · Apr 29, 2026

Android. Private preview. Live.

Tap to Add launched in private preview on April 29, 2026 inside the Mobile Payment Element on Android. Documented publicly at docs.stripe.com/payments/mobile/embedded-tap-to-add.

Merchants invited into the preview can integrate today; iOS support depends on Apple approving the use case, with conversations re-opening once Android proves the value.

The Reflection · The Lessons

The Lessons.

01
Infrastructure can be repointed, not just built.

The Tap to Pay stack was already there. The work was seeing what else it could do.

02
The strongest design moves are economic, not visual.

The interaction looks simple. The auth-rate, fraud, and liability shifts behind it are anything but.

03
Sole designer doesn't mean solo work.

Cross-team alignment with Terminal was the highest-leverage thing I did this project.

04
Private preview is a discipline.

You ship the right slice, document what's deferred, and protect the work from scope creep.
