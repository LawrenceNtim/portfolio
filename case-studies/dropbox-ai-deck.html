Process · Problem Framing

Design doc.

The first artifact. Before any pixel, the problem had to hold up on its own: written in plain language, scoped to what AI could actually do for Dropbox users today.

Problem Framing Card
Dropbox AI Product Problem Statement

Dropbox users struggle to find and take advantage of their content today. With the advent of AI, can we find easier ways for them to utilize their content?

User Problem

When users come to Dropbox to retrieve content it is because they either

1) Need to take an action with that content (e.g. share), or

2) Are looking for a piece of information within their content.

• Our existing retrieval mechanisms only support (1).

• To meet (2) we have an opportunity to leverage AI to allow a user to get additional context on their files without having to go in and out of files searching for it.

Hypothesis

We believe that building a better retrieval experience for DocSend users, using AI will reduce churn and achieve a higher percentage of users moving to AI powered SKU's. We will know we are successful when we see reductions in cancellations and increases in upgrades.

AI – Customer Perspective & Opportunities Summary
Latest Update: June 15, 2023 · Questions: @Javier V, @Alexandra B
TLDR

The inevitability of AI is clear to customers, and they recognize the life-changing inflection point it represents. Interest and concerns exist; because of this, companies have a limited runway to clearly establish how and why they'll be leveraging AI in what they offer.

Customers need help reconciling their perception of Dropbox as trusted and secure with the unknowns of AI. This represents Dropbox's biggest challenge and biggest opportunity.

Finally, when we say "AI first," we should not lose sight of what our customers want: to have their workflow needs met and business outcomes improved. Because of this, it's important to reinforce that AI is an integral ingredient in how we work against that.

Topline Findings:

Workflow efficiency is the "ultimate benefit" of AI. Customers want AI to help reduce mundane, repetitive, administrative tasks so they can focus more on the value-add work.

Sentiment about AI is positive but cautious. Customers across company sizes and levels of AI familiarity express a mix of optimism and concern regarding AI.

[Preliminary Findings] Q&A in Search Research
August 2023 · @Margaret L

On August 21–August 23rd, we conducted a remote moderated usability study with 7 Dropbox customers (2 BUSINESS, 4 PLUS, 1 PRO). We had a mix of customers when it comes to familiarity and usage of AI tools: 4 novice users (no or very little prior knowledge and usage of AI tools) and 3 experienced users (used AI tools prior this study). Only 6 out of 7 participants were able to interact with the feature due to a SEV. Participants were asked about their current usage of Dropbox and retrieval process. Later, they were prompted to the Dropbox AI tool in their Dropbox account and were asked to explore and interact with it while thinking aloud.

🌷 Key Findings

🟢 Willingness to try

• 7 out of 7 users were comfortable using Q&A in Search on their Dropbox account. 1 out of 7 users was unable to test the feature due to a SEV.

• 6 out of 6 users claimed that they would be interested in trying Q&A in Search after interacting with it

AI Understanding

• Participants had varying levels of understanding and expectations regarding AI capabilities, ranging from skepticism to trust.

• Skepticism didn't come from whether they can trust AI to read their content, but whether they can trust the solutions the AI was offering

• 5 out of 6 users reported still wanting to review source material to verify the answers provided by AI

Process · Design: Entry points

Iteration: AI Right Rail.

Persistent panel on the right. Discoverable but heavy — a constant reminder that AI was watching.

Always available, always visible. The panel offered "Pick up where you left off" and "Suggested next steps" the moment a user logged in.

But the team found that always-on AI read as always-watching. The space the panel occupied competed with the actual work surface.

Process · Design: Entry points

Iteration: Search Toggle.

AI as a toggle inside the search bar. Out of the way until summoned — but the toggle itself was a tax on every search.

A toggle next to the search input. AI off by default, opt-in per query. Quieter than the right rail — but it asked the user to opt into AI before they'd seen it work.

The decision moment came before the value, not after. Adoption suffered.

Process · Design: Entry points

Result. Keeping things simple.

The shipped pattern lives where the user is already looking — in the search result itself. No panel to ignore, no toggle to flip. The answer is where the file would have been.

No new place to learn, no new place to dismiss. AI shows up where the question gets asked.

Process · Design: Entry points

Near-term future: Integrated experience.

Beyond search and summary: AI as an active morning assistant. A daily brief, highest-priority items, the ability to ask anything about your content from a single prompt.

The shipped pattern earned the right to extend. The future of AI in Dropbox is integrated, not modular.

Solutions · AI Guidelines

Trust and Safety: Six AI Principles.

When the trust crisis ended, we extracted what we'd learned into a set of guidelines we now design every AI feature against.

Collaborated with PM, Legal
01

Leverage AI to serve our customers.

We use AI when it helps us deliver better experiences for our customers. At no point will we use it as a means to sell customer data.

02

Keep customers in control of their data.

Customer trust and the privacy of their data are our foundation. We will not use customer data to train AI models without consent.

03

Be transparent about how we use AI.

We are committed to transparency with our customers. We will provide clear explanations of how our AI experiences work so that our customers understand how these technologies can benefit them.

04

Respect people, their safety, and their rights.

We will work to ensure that our AI innovations not only serve people, but also respect their rights and their safety.

05

Be accountable to our customers.

We will continuously seek feedback from our customers about our AI-powered experiences, and ensure these experiences remain under human direction.

06

Champion fairness in AI technology.

We are committed to inclusiveness, non-discrimination, and fairness. We will strive to limit bias in our AI technologies, and ensure that they are
reliable and robust.

The Reflection · The Lessons

Four things I learned in public, that I do differently now.

01
Over-communicate.

When the work is novel, the team is small, and the timeline is tight, the bottleneck isn't decisions, it's shared context. I now write the working brief weekly, post the open questions before standup, and treat "did I say this twice" as a feature, not a tax.

02
Tighter team loops.

Big XFN reviews are the wrong unit of work for AI. I run smaller, faster, three-person loops now (designer, PM, engineer) and bring the wider room in only when there's a decision to make, not a decision to socialize.

03
New patterns, on purpose.

AI products invite reuse of chat patterns that don't fit the work. I now start every AI surface with the user's existing task and ask what shape AI takes inside that task. Not what shape AI takes when it's the task itself.

04
Even more HCD.

Trust is a research problem before it's a UI problem. I now bring research into AI work earlier than feels comfortable, and I push the bar on disclosure and consent design specifically, because the cost of getting it wrong compounds faster than any other surface.
