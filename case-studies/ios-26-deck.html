Process · The tension

Apple's material. The merchant's brand.

Liquid Glass wants to be see-through and borrow its color from whatever sits behind it. A merchant's checkout wants to be their color, every time, on every phone. And someone had to decide who flips the switch.

Liquid GlassApple's material Appearance APIThe merchant's brand
Surface Translucent Opaque
Color Borrowed from the content beneath A brand hex, set once
Owner Apple, per OS release The merchant, per app release
Turned on by Apple, for every app on iOS 26 The merchant, when they choose
Moves under you Yes. iOS 27 adds a translucency slider for users. Only when the merchant ships
The crux
Opt in, or opt out?

Nobody knew yet whether merchants would want glass by default. The answer decided whose checkout changed on launch day.

Why not opt out?

Glass on for everyone rewrites every merchant's theme the day they update the SDK, whether their brand survives the glass or not.

Why not wait?

The sheet reads as iOS 18 inside an iOS 26 app, and Apple's floating controls land on top of it anyway.

Process · The reposition

Opt in, don't overwrite.

I stopped asking which material wins. I asked who gets to decide.

Liquid Glass ships as a layer on top of the Appearance API. The merchant turns it on with one call, and it updates padding, heights, corner radii, and blur. Their colors, fonts, and brand stay theirs.

Minimal changes to respect Liquid Glass first, small refinements after launch. Public preview let us watch the iOS ecosystem before we pick a default.

Apple sets the material. The merchant flips the switch.

Solutions.

Solutions · Design: Sheet & material

The sheet, pulled apart.

One call, applyLiquidGlass, reaches every layer of the sheet. It updates padding, heights, corner radii and blur at once, so the merchant's app stays alive underneath, glass carries the material, and card data sits on solid rows that never inherit the blur.

I designed the Liquid Glass system across every layer and surface. We, John Woo and the iOS team, built it into the API and shipped it.

  1. 01BackdropMerchant app, untouched by the call
  2. 02MaterialNew blur, merchant brand via the Appearance API
  3. 03ContentSolid rows, new padding, heights and corner radii
  4. 04ControlsPay and close, always on top
Solutions · Design: Inputs & legibility

Card data, legible over anything.

The card form is where Liquid Glass has to behave. With applyLiquidGlass on, field groups get new heights and rounder corners, while the fields themselves stay solid so card numbers never sit on the blur.

I designed the input; we shipped it across PaymentSheet, Payment Element, CustomerSheet and AddressElement.

Future Still open from the ship note: input heights, roundness of input fields, text contrast and edge cases. Public preview is where we tune them.

Lessons · Chapter 05

Lessons.

Lessons · The lessons Draft

The lessons.

01
A system redesign is a brand problem.

Apple changed the material. The hard part was keeping Stripe, and every merchant on top of it, recognizable inside it.

02
Design the fallbacks first.

Reduce Transparency and Increase Contrast are not edge cases. If the sheet holds with the glass turned off, it holds everywhere.

03
Glass is for chrome, not content.

Let the frame float. Keep card numbers, totals, and the pay button on solid ground where people read them.

04
Design ahead of a moving beta.

Every seed changed something. I made decisions that could absorb a new blur value without a redesign.

Lessons · Future · iOS 27 Future

Glass, on a slider.

iOS 27 lets people choose how much glass they want. So the sheet can't be designed at one setting. It has to hold at every point between clear and tinted.
